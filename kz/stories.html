Account 01 · Personal memory

A name from my childhood

I first remember the word “Nohrikha” from when I was about five years old. I heard it often in our family, particularly from my grandfather.

“We should go to Babka Nohrikha,” he would say whenever someone fell ill, needed some kind of cure, or asked a question nobody knew how to answer. Sometimes he sounded serious; at other times, there was a hint of humour: “Go and ask Babka Nohrikha.”

I never took it to mean an actual visit to a particular place. In our conversations, Nohrikha was more like a final authority, someone who might have an answer to an impossible question. But I kept wondering: who was she?

When I asked relatives, they remembered the expression but could not explain where it came from. Even my grandfather’s children did not know who Nohrikha was. Nobody had thought to ask him while there was still time.

My grandfather was born in 1939 to a family of Volga Germans. During the Second World War, the family was deported and ended up in the Urals. Some relatives later lived in Kazakhstan. Yet I could find no clear explanation of how he had learned about Nohrikha.

Around 2005, I searched for the name online for the first time—and found absolutely nothing: no story, definition, or mention of anyone with that nickname.

I returned to the search many times. Around 2016, I looked at the search-query statistics available from Google and Yandex to see whether anyone else had searched for the word.

In the statistics I could access, there were no visible queries. It felt as if no one but me had ever typed the word into a search engine.

How many words can you think of that do not seem to exist on the internet at all? I could not think of one. Then I had one: Nohrikha.

Around 2021, something finally appeared: a play titled “Psovaya okhota” (“Hound Hunting”), published several years earlier. A character mentioned Babka Nohrikha as someone who knew how to heal. Both the name and its meaning were startlingly close to what I remembered from childhood.

I tried to contact the author, leaving a message on a literary website and reaching out through social networks. For years I heard nothing. In August 2026, at last, a reply arrived.

What mattered most was that another family remembered Babka Nohrikha as a real woman, not a character from folklore. They knew her by that very nickname. Her surname was Nokhrina, but nobody remembered her first name.

According to their family accounts, she assisted women in childbirth, lived in Kazakhstan, and spent some years in China. She had a life and a history of her own.

The question that started it all remains unanswered: where, when, and through whom did the memory of her reach my grandfather?

This website exists to preserve these memories and, perhaps, find an answer.

Account 02 · Family memories

A story from another family

Another family preserved memories of a woman everyone called Babka Nohrikha. These stories passed from one generation to the next. Much of what survived came from a woman who was born in China in 1942.

Her family had lived through dispossession, hunger and difficult years connected with the construction of the Turkestan–Siberian Railway. Before leaving for China, they lived in Kazakhstan. Family recollections place a woman named Nokhrina nearby, in the village of Bolshaya Bukon, Kokpektinsky District, Semipalatinsk Region. Everyone called her “Babka Nohrikha”; her first name was no longer remembered.

The family travelled to China together with Nohrikha and later returned to Kazakhstan with her. A girl born in China in 1942 would grow up to tell her own children about this remarkable woman.

Nohrikha was remembered as a skilled midwife. She was often away from home, helping women give birth. Families in nomadic settlements waited for her when babies were due. She reportedly spoke Uyghur and Kazakh fluently. Relatives remembered her as someone whose care brought mothers and newborns safely through childbirth.

They said that Nohrikha could neither read nor write, not even sign her name. Yet, according to those stories, she passed an examination in midwifery, worked officially in the field, and others came to learn from her. Her calm, wisdom, and skill were what people remembered.

Around 1954, it seems, the family and Nohrikha returned from China to Kazakhstan. Their paths then diverged: the relatives settled in Beskaragay, Pavlodar Region, while Nohrikha apparently remained in Bolshaya Bukon. By then she was remembered as an older woman.

Another person appears in these memories: a teacher who set up a school for Russian-speaking children in China, finding paper and stitching notebooks herself. She knew Nohrikha well, was said to be about the same age, and later settled in Semipalatinsk.

The family continued living in Pavlodar Region until the mid-to-late 1970s. Most of the relatives subsequently moved to Latvia.

That is how the story of a real woman named Nokhrina survived. We do not know whether she ever met the grandfather in the other family. But both families remembered the same rare nickname—Babka Nohrikha—and a woman whom people trusted when they needed help.

The literary work that prompted the search is “Psovaya okhota” ↗ (in Russian).
